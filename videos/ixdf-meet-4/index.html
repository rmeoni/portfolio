IxDF Guatemala City Meet #4: Accessibility in Guatemala
Format
Virtual Masterclass
Host
Rodrigo Meoño
Country Manager @ IxDF Guatemala City


Designing for more than 25 cultures: that's the accessibility challenge in Guatemala. In this Interaction Design Foundation (IxDF) Guatemala City Meet #4 session, I walked the local UX community through why accessibility deserves more attention in Guatemala than in almost any other market. Starting from the classic UX honeycomb — useful, usable, findable, credible, desirable, accessible, and valuable — I explained that accessibility means any person, regardless of ability, should be able to use what we design, and that equity means adjusting our tools so everyone, not just the majority, can actually reach the same outcome. From there we dug into Google's "Next Billion Users" framework and a set of socioeconomic statistics that make Guatemala's design landscape uniquely challenging, before opening the floor to an interactive discussion and a collaborative Jamboard exercise on how local products could become more accessible.

About the Session

The goal of the talk was to show that accessibility isn't a checkbox — it's a direct response to who our users actually are. I shared the quantitative research process I use with every client, in Guatemala or abroad, before designing a single screen: gathering hard demographic data on literacy, language, income, banking access, and disability rates before making assumptions about how someone will use a product. We then opened the floor to the group — UX designers, developers, and team leads from across Guatemala — to share real examples of apps and everyday technology that got accessibility right, and ones that clearly didn't.

Guatemala by the Numbers

These are some of the statistics I shared to ground the conversation in data rather than assumptions:

  • Population & language: 18 million people, roughly 70% of whom speak Spanish. The country officially recognizes 25 languages — Spanish, 22 Mayan languages, Xinca, and Garífuna — spoken by about 6 million people who don't primarily communicate in Spanish.
  • Literacy: An estimated 83% adult literacy rate, meaning roughly 1 in 5 adults — around 3 million people — still can't read or write.
  • Education: Only 54.6% of adults over 25 completed primary school, 33.9% completed lower secondary, and just 5.1% hold a university degree.
  • Connectivity: About 20.4 million mobile connections for the population, but only around 56–60% internet penetration and roughly 10.4 million active social media accounts.
  • Economy & banking: 56% of the population lives in poverty (16.2% in extreme poverty), and only 65% of adults have a bank account.
  • Disability: 22.4% of adults 15 and older report some functional difficulty — vision, mobility, self-care, or cognitive — roughly 3.5 million people, not a small edge case.
  • Health: 1 in every 2 children in Guatemala suffers some degree of chronic malnutrition, which can affect long-term cognitive development.

Key Takeaways for Accessible Design

  • Design for low literacy first: pair every critical flow with clear iconography, plain language, and text-to-speech so users who can't read fluently can still complete a task.
  • Treat multi-language support as core, not a stretch goal — starting with the most widely spoken Mayan languages instead of defaulting to Spanish-only.
  • Follow WCAG color contrast guidelines and offer both light and dark modes — not just for aesthetics, but for users with light sensitivity.
  • Use real, visible form labels outside the input instead of placeholder-only text, so screen readers and low-literacy users alike can understand what's being asked.
  • Add proper alt text and aria-labels to every icon and image — voice-over tools read the code, not the pixels.
  • Ship icons and logos as SVGs instead of compressed JPGs/PNGs, so they stay legible when a user zooms in instead of pixelating.
  • Use inclusive, person-first language when referring to users with different abilities, rather than labeling people by a disability.

Examples From the Community

During the discussion, attendees shared real Guatemalan examples on both ends of the spectrum: self-checkout kiosks at a major supermarket chain that speak instructions aloud as you scan; a utility company's bill-payment app praised for being simple and functional; and products like Duolingo cited as accessible by design. On the other side, parking payment machines and gym check-in kiosks came up repeatedly as confusing, inconsistent experiences that force users to relearn the interaction every single time.
